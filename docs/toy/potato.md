# Potato

The potato is a starchy tuber domesticated in the Andes and now grown on every inhabited continent, and this short document sketches where it came from and which cultivars matter.

## History

The potato originated in the highlands of the Andes, where indigenous peoples in what is now southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia domesticated wild Solanum species into a staple crop, cultivating them for thousands of years before European contact. Spanish conquistadors encountered the potato during their conquest of the Inca Empire in the 1530s and carried it back across the Atlantic, with its first documented arrival in Europe traditionally dated to 1570, when shipments reached Spain. For decades afterward the potato remained a curiosity, grown mostly in botanical gardens and monastery plots, and viewed with suspicion by many Europeans, who distrusted its status as a relative of nightshade and associated it with disease and poverty. Adoption spread slowly through Spain, Italy, and the Low Countries over the following century, gaining ground first as animal fodder and only gradually as human food. By the eighteenth century, deliberate promotion by figures such as Frederick the Great of Prussia and Antoine-Augustin Parmentier in France helped overcome popular resistance, and the potato became a dietary staple across much of Europe, prized for its high caloric yield per acre and its resilience in poor soils and cold climates. Cultivation expanded further into Eastern Europe, Russia, and especially Ireland, where by the early nineteenth century it had become the primary food source for much of the rural population, a dependence that set the stage for the catastrophic famine of the 1840s. From Europe, traders, colonists, and migrants carried the potato onward to North America, Asia, and Africa, completing its transformation from an obscure Andean tuber into one of the world's most widely grown food crops.

## Varieties

Thousands of potato cultivars exist, but a handful account for most of what reaches kitchens and farms. Russet Burbank, the long brown-skinned potato behind most French fries, is prized for its high starch content and fluffy texture when baked. Yukon Gold, with buttery yellow flesh, is a genuine all-purpose potato that roasts, mashes, and boils equally well. Kennebec, a smooth tan-skinned workhorse, delivers reliable yields for growers and crisp results for fryers. Fingerling potatoes, small and knobby with firm, waxy flesh, are prized for roasting whole and holding their shape. Vitelotte, a deep-purple French heirloom, adds striking color and a nutty flavor to salads and specialty plates. Desiree, with pink-red skin and pale yellow flesh, is a popular salad potato that stays firm after boiling. Bintje, a pale yellow Dutch cultivar, remains a European staple for chips and everyday cooking. Beneath this variety lies a simpler grouping: floury (or mealy) potatoes such as Russet Burbank have high starch and low moisture, so they break down into a light, fluffy texture suited to baking and frying, while waxy potatoes such as Fingerlings and Desiree have lower starch and hold their shape through boiling, making them better for salads and gratins; skin color, from tan and gold through red and deep purple, further distinguishes cultivars but does not by itself predict how a potato cooks.
